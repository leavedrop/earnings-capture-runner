Evolution is Essential One of the most fundamental requirements for long-term success is the ability to evolve in response to a changing environment. In nature, survival and progress depend on adaptation. Through the process of evolution, organisms become better suited to their environment, with their strengths becoming more prevalent over successive generations. As environmental conditions change, so too must the characteristics that enable organisms to survive and thrive. Evolution, therefore, is not simply a process of improvement, but a continuous process of adaptation to remain relevant and viable. The same principle applies to business. Markets evolve, customer expectations shift, technologies advance, and competitive landscapes are constantly reshaped. What drives success today may not be what sustains it tomorrow. Businesses that remain anchored to the conditions, strategies, and capabilities that once made them successful risk losing their relevance as the world around them changes. Long-term success, therefore, depends not only on the ability to perform well in the present, but on the willingness and capacity to continually adapt, transform, and evolve for the future. The ability to evolve is one of the defining characteristics of businesses that endure and thrive over generations. It is the difference between becoming Berkshire Hathaway, or becoming Kodak Cameras. Recall that Amazon started as a bookseller, Alphabet was once only Google, Meta began as Facebook, and NVIDIA used its chips largely for gaming applications. As for Brookfield, over the past 40+ years, we have adhered to the same very basic principles of investing — which are to invest to earn cash flows, with good people and partners, in good countries, and in world-class assets. Despite a consistent approach to our business over this period, we have continually evolved our strategy as the world has changed. This is exemplified by the fact that approximately 50% of our total $1.3 trillion of assets under management are in asset classes that did not exist at investable scale 15 years ago. Our business has evolved across the board — in our corporate form, capital structure, and investment sectors. Our funding model has evolved through the building of our alternative asset management business in the 2000s, the launch of our public affiliates in the 2010s, our insurance business in 2020, and our current focus on expanding access to private markets for individual investors. Our corporate structure has also changed as we created new businesses and adapted to changing capital markets. Our investment focus has also evolved as infrastructure investment has expanded into new areas, including ai and the buildout of its supporting infrastructure, but the same is true of energy, alternative real estate, batteries, and telecom towers. Over 20 years, we have also moved ever so slowly into the infrastructure supporting technology but given that the physical world is now colliding head-on with technology, we are now at the heart of building the physical backbone that enables it, providing energy and compute infrastructure to the best companies globally and using that knowledge to invest around these themes. This also has the added benefit of allowing us to be at the forefront of using technology to make our businesses better. It is the ability to continually adapt and evolve (not wholesale transformation) that makes the difference between good and great (or not so great) companies. Wholesale transformations work once in a while, but more often large mistakes are made during the process due to incorrect assumptions or flawed execution. On the other hand, a series of smaller, deliberate changes allow a company to adapt and learn over time. As a result, if successfully executed, this approach has a far greater chance of success. 6    BROOKFIELD CORPORATION Organizations cannot stop the world from changing. The best one can do is continually evolve. Great companies change before they need to. Lucky companies manage to adjust when the world turns against them and survive. The rest are left behind and eventually become just a name in history. The Best is Yet to Come for Westinghouse The electricity markets of most countries today are strained due to electrification of industry, lack of new-build generation capacity, reliance on foreign inputs, and years of neglect of grids. Solar, wind, gas, and other power sources are each important but cannot provide the same benefits as nuclear power. There is no other source of power that can be built which is baseload (runs all hours), can be built at scale (a two-unit nuclear plant produces ~2,300 MW), is carbon-free, can store fuel for up to five years on-site (enabling energy security and reliability unlike anything else) and can last up to 80 years (as close to perpetual as energy gets). Nuclear plants currently form the critical backbone of energy infrastructure in many countries and are very critical to their reliability and resilience. But to meet the demands of electrification, energy security, and the buildout of ai infrastructure, more global nuclear capacity is needed. Westinghouse technology is used by roughly half of the world's operating nuclear reactors. The company also services over half of the global reactor fleet, with the majority of its cash flows today generated from recurring fuel supply and maintenance services. Our new Westinghouse AP1000 technology builds on decades of safety records and, with the scaling up of manufacturing, can now be built at a scale, which should bring down both costs and construction timelines. Westinghouse is the design and engineering behind many of the most advanced and proven large-scale newly constructed reactors globally and it does not appear that anyone else will ever surpass them. Westinghouse has built six new reactors in the last few years, and the technology is operating at historically high levels of efficiency and safety. Rarely have we owned a business that governments want to ensure are successful because it is critical to their country’s success. In particular, the U.S. government has been extremely helpful to Westinghouse in order to bring nuclear power online faster and at lower cost. Building on our strategic partnership with the U.S. Department of Commerce announced last year, the U.S. Department of Energy announced a $17.5 billion commitment to finance the procurement of long-lead-time equipment for a further ten new large-scale reactors. By supporting early equipment procurement, this should materially lower execution risk, shorten development timelines, and establish a repeatable model for large-scale nuclear construction in the U.S. and around the world. It is already making a difference. More recently, Westinghouse agreed to a multi-faceted deal with the Korean and U.S. governments. Korea will invest up to $120 billion into reactors constructed on U.S. federal lands. This is substantial progress on our deal announced last year and further brings the needed historic levels of new-build nuclear construction closer to reality. Second, Korea has agreed to bring its supply chain to the U.S. to scale up production of plants. We need more talented construction groups, and Korea has some excellent ones. Third, we will allow Korea to build one set of its own reactors, on a one-time exemption basis, in the U.S., and Westinghouse will earn enhanced licensing fees for this build as their technology uses Westinghouse's intellectual property, which Korean companies are currently not permitted to use in the U.S. In total, Westinghouse has 14 reactors in various phases of construction, another 40 coming, and 80+ behind that. This is one of the largest buildouts of energy ever. This also does not include various other countries that are also restarting discussions with Westinghouse to build nuclear in their countries, given the benefits it provides to their energy systems. This follows earlier waves including railways, automobiles, electricity, telephones, computers, internet, and mobile devices. What is clear is that we are in the midst of a transformation of how we live and do business. What is never clear is exactly how and when this will affect people and businesses. There are many things that will not be recognizable in the future, and surely people will look back and find many of today’s activities quaint. This has been the case with all the above transformations, but the difference this time is that the speed and scale at which this is occurring are on another level. What is less clear today is who will make money, how they will make it, and whether we are building too fast. These questions pose the greatest investment opportunity and threat of our lifetimes. It is the sheer scale and speed at which this is happening that creates the challenge and opportunity. The large language model (LLM) companies have decided that they need to move fast or they will be left behind. Whether this is true is the $5-10 trillion question. One thing is very clear this time: access to capital will be the ultimate decider in this story, and those who have it will win. The good news is that this revolution is being led by companies that are as large as countries. This is very comforting, as these companies each have very profitable core businesses and are reinvesting profits from those businesses into this LLM race — and they can afford to do it. They may not like the outcome if it does not work out for them, but they will be fine financially. To remind you, many of the greatest businesses today went for years/decades without being profitable, but today they generate hundreds of billions in revenue. As an example, most television, radio, magazine, and other forms of advertising have transferred onto online platforms. This industry, which was worth zero at launch, has captured up to $5 trillion of value. At Brookfield, on a selective basis, this backdrop offers some outstanding opportunities to earn excellent returns without taking undue risk. Importantly, we approach these opportunities no differently than we have approached investments for decades. We focus on assets backed by long-term contracts with high-quality counterparties, where we take limited or no technology risk and can earn attractive risk-adjusted returns. We have decades of proven skills in building critical digital infrastructure, generating and connecting energy, raising large sums of money, and building relationships with the strongest in the world. As a result, our counterparty risk is very low when providing power, data centers, or compute to these technology titans, and the returns we are able to generate are good. This is possible because not many have our scale, not many can put together the capital required, and not many have the ability to get the assets built. We have all three. While many can commit capital to large projects, actually developing and building them is very difficult. It requires experience, operating expertise, and execution capability. We have spent decades building these capabilities across our businesses, and the combination of our investment expertise and operating capabilities allows us not only to identify and secure the best opportunities, but also to deliver them at scale. This has led, and continues to lead, to us being able to invest tens of billions into opportunities with very strong risk-adjusted returns. In our own businesses, we are also in the midst of deploying these LLM technologies to enhance revenues, reduce costs, scale businesses and transform manufacturing. It is this deployment that is required to justify the expenditure on the LLM models and the intelligence they offer users. Our early use is powerful, and we are clearly only getting started. This is very impactful for our business as margins could improve not by 10%, but by quantums of that. Carry eligible capital represents the capital committed, pledged or invested in the private funds that we manage and which entitle us to earn carried interest. The Corporation retains 100% of the carried interest earned on mature funds and is entitled to receive 33.3% of the carried interest on new funds of our Asset Management business. Carry eligible capital includes both invested and uninvested (i.e., uncalled) private fund amounts as well as those amounts invested directly by investors (co-investments) if those entitle us to earn carried interest. We believe this measure is useful to investors as it provides additional insight into the capital base upon which we have potential to earn carried interest once minimum investment returns are sufficiently assured. Carried interest is a contractual arrangement whereby we receive a fixed percentage of profits generated within a private fund provided that the investors receive a predetermined minimum return. Carried interest is typically paid towards the end of the life of a fund after the capital has been returned to investors and may be subject to “clawback” until all investments have been monetized and minimum investment returns are sufficiently assured. Realized carried interest is an IFRS measure and represents our share of investment returns based on realized gains within a private fund. Realized carried interest earned is recognized when an underlying investment is profitably disposed of and the fund’s cumulative returns are in excess of preferred returns, in accordance with the respective terms set out in the fund’s governing agreements, and when the probability of clawback is remote. Realized carried interest is determined on third-party capital that is no longer subject to future investment performance. These metrics are used by our Chief Operating Decision Maker in assessing operating results and the performance of our businesses on a segmented basis. Our segment financial measures are defined as follows: i.    Distributable Earnings DE from our Asset Management segment is defined as the earnings received by the Corporation that are available for distribution to common shareholders or to be reinvested in the business. It is calculated as the sum of distributable earnings from our Asset Management business and realized carried interest, net of equity-based compensation costs. DE from our Asset Management segment includes fees, net of the associated costs, that we earn from managing capital in our perpetual affiliates, private funds and liquid strategies accounts. We are also eligible to earn incentive payments in the form of incentive distributions, performance fees or carried interest. Our Asset Management segment distributes substantially all of its distributable earnings as a dividend to its shareholders; Q3 2026 INTERIM REPORT    75 therefore, DE represents our profitability from our Asset Management segment. We do not use DE as a measure of cash generated from our operations. DE from our Wealth Solutions segment is calculated as our share of equity accounted net income from our Wealth Solutions segment, excluding the impact of depreciation and amortization, deferred income taxes, net income from our equity accounted investments, mark-to-market on investments and derivatives, breakage and transaction costs, and is inclusive of our proportionate share of DE from investments in associates. ii.    Net Operating Income NOI from our Real Estate segment is defined as: i) property-specific revenues from our commercial properties operations less direct commercial property expenses before the impact of depreciation and amortization; and ii) revenues from our hospitality operations less direct hospitality expenses before the impact of depreciation and amortization. NOI represents an income-generating property’s profitability before adding costs from financing or taxes, and is a strong indication of our Real Estate business’ ability to impact the operating performance of its properties through proactive management and leasing. Depreciation and capital expenditures are excluded from NOI as we believe that the value of most of our properties typically increases over time, provided we make the necessary maintenance expenditures, the timing and magnitude of which may differ from the amount of depreciation recorded in any given period. We do not use NOI as a measure of cash generated from our operations. iii.    Funds from Operations We define FFO from our Corporate Activities segment and our Operating Businesses, excluding the Real Estate business, as net income excluding fair value changes, depreciation and amortization and deferred income taxes, net of non-controlling interests. When determining FFO, we include our proportionate share of the FFO from equity accounted investments on a fully diluted basis. FFO also includes realized disposition gains and losses, which are gains or losses arising from transactions during the reporting period, adjusted to include associated fair value changes and revaluation surplus recorded in prior periods, taxes payable or receivable in connection with those transactions and amounts that are recorded directly in equity, such as ownership changes. FFO represents the company’s share of revenues less costs incurred within our operations, which include interest expenses and other costs. Specifically, it includes the impact of contracts that we enter into to generate revenues, including power sales agreements, contracts that our operating businesses enter into such as leases and take or pay contracts and sales of inventory. FFO includes the impact of changes in leverage or the cost of that financial leverage and other costs incurred to operate our business. We use realized disposition gains and losses within FFO in order to provide additional insight regarding the performance of investments on a cumulative realized basis, including any unrealized fair value adjustments that were recorded in equity and not otherwise reflected in current period FFO, and believe it is useful to investors to better understand variances between reporting periods. We exclude depreciation and amortization from FFO as we believe that the value of most of our assets typically increases over time, provided we make the necessary maintenance expenditures, the timing and magnitude of which may differ from the amount of depreciation recorded in any given period. In addition, the depreciated cost base of our assets is reflected in the ultimate realized disposition gain or loss on disposal. As noted above, unrealized fair value changes are excluded from FFO until the period in which the asset is sold. We also exclude deferred income taxes from FFO because the vast majority of the company’s deferred income tax assets and liabilities are a result of the revaluation of our assets under IFRS Accounting Standards. Our definition of FFO differs from the definition used by other organizations, as well as the definition of FFO used by the Real Property Association of Canada (“REALPAC”) and the National Association of Real Estate Investment Trusts, Inc. (“NAREIT”), in part because the NAREIT definition is based on U.S. Generally Accepted Accounting Principles (“U.S. GAAP”), as opposed to IFRS Accounting Standards. The key differences between our definition of FFO and the determination of FFO by REALPAC and/or NAREIT are that we include the following: realized disposition gains or losses and cash taxes payable or receivable on those gains or losses, if any; foreign exchange gains or losses on monetary items not forming part of our net investment in foreign operations; and foreign exchange gains or losses on the sale of an investment in a foreign operation. We do not use FFO as a measure of cash generated from our operations. We illustrate how we reconcile the financial measure for each operating segment to net income in Note 3(c)(ii) and 3(c)(iii) of the consolidated financial statements. Share-Based Compensation The company and its consolidated subsidiaries account for stock options using the fair value method. Under the fair value method, compensation expense for stock options that are direct awards of stock is measured at fair value at the grant date using an option pricing model and recognized over the vesting period. Options issued under the company’s Management Share Option Plan (“MSOP”) generally vest over a period of up to five years, expire 10 years after the grant date, and are settled through issuance of Class A shares.
